# GitHub bot and automated-account detection: verified catalogue for the SOTA table

Verification note: every source cited below was opened on 2026-11-10 (full-text PDFs or preprints, read directly or by text extraction plus keyword search; where only a landing page or abstract was read, that is stated). Numbers carry their location in the source. "Local check" marks an aggregate computation I ran on the project's own GH Archive dump 2024-04-01-15 (no logins printed or transmitted); these are not literature facts. ## 1. Which methods exist? (BoDeGHa, BIMAN, BotHunter, RABBIT and 2023-2026 successors)

### Takeaway
Account-level GitHub bot detection is a small literature of supervised classifiers trained on curated label lists (BIMAN 2020, BoDeGHa 2021, BotHunter 2022, BIMBAS/RABBIT 2024-2025, BotHawk and BDGOA 2023). On the one shared test set in the literature, BotHunter leads (weighted F1 .950) but falls to .787 without name features, while activity-sequence BIMBAS/RABBIT reaches .899. The prior art closest to the disappearance task is the GitHub abuse line (Du et al. 2020 on GH Archive; StarScout, ICSE 2026), which uses platform removal only for validation; none of these works does PU learning or predicts future disappearance.

#### BoDeGHa (Golzadeh, Decan, Legay, Mens, 2021)
- Venue: Journal of Systems and Software 175, article 110911, May 2021; DOI 10.1016/j.jss.2021.110911 — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jss.2021.110911), [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- Task: classify each commenter of a given repository as bot or human from issue and PR comments — [preprint, section 1 and 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- Data: Libraries.io Jan-2020 dump, 136,529 randomly selected repositories; last 100 comments of the last 100 issues/PRs extracted on 16 Feb 2020 via GraphQL; 10,874,611 comments spanning 17 Dec 2009-15 Feb 2020 (Table 1; the text says 837K distinct contributors while Table 1 says 873,489). After requiring at least 10 comments: 79,342 commenters. Labelled subset: 5,082 commenters (4,644 random plus 438 added because likely bots, 386 of them via name substrings such as "bot", "ci", "cla", "auto"); final 5,000 commenters, 527 bots and 4,473 humans, after excluding 78 "mixed" and 4 undecided; labels by manual rating with account names hidden, Cohen's kappa 0.96 (Table 2, section 3.3) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- Features and model: number of comments, empty comments, comment patterns (DBSCAN on a combined Levenshtein and Jaccard distance) and Gini inequality between patterns; Random Forest (entropy, 10 trees, depth 10), best of 91 grid-searched classifiers, 60/40 stratified split, cost-sensitive (sections 4, 5.1, 10) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- Metrics: test set of 2,000 (211 bots, 1,789 humans): bots P .94, R .91, F1 .92; humans .99/.99/.99; weighted .98/.98/.98; TP 192, FN 19, FP 13, TN 1,776 (Table 6). F1 rises from 0.87 (bin 0-4 non-empty comments) and stabilises at 0.96-1.00 from 10 non-empty comments (section 5.2, Fig. 7) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- Limitations stated: no claim for accounts with fewer than 10 comments; at most 100 comments considered; "mixed" accounts excluded; no generalisation claim to other activities or platforms (section 8); bots that copy human text and humans with repetitive comments are misclassified (section 7) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- Availability: replication package [IdentifyBots_ReplicationPackage](https://github.com/mehdigolzadeh/IdentifyBots_ReplicationPackage); dataset [Zenodo 4000388](https://zenodo.org/records/4000388) (CC-BY-4.0 per Zenodo API); see question 5 for tool status.

#### BIMAN (Dey, Mousavi, Ponce, Fry, Vasilescu, Filippova, Mockus, 2020)
- Venue: MSR 2020, pp. 209-219; DOI 10.1145/3379597.3387478 — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3379597.3387478), [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172).
- Task: decide whether a commit author identity (name+email) is a bot, and characterise bots — [preprint, abstract and section 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172).
- Data: World of Code version P (collected 15 May-5 Jun 2019): 34,424,362 author IDs, 1,685,985,529 commits; golden dataset of 13,150 bots found by the name regex BIN and manually verified plus 13,150 random humans; ensemble trained on 134 authors (67 known bots, 67 humans) (sections 4.1, 4.2.1, 4.2.4) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172).
- Features and model: BIN (name/email regex), BIM (commit-message template score, alignment threshold 40%), BICA (Random Forest on 6 file/project features, Table 1), combined by a Random Forest ensemble — [preprint, section 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172).
- Metrics: ensemble AUC-ROC 0.89-0.95, median 0.90 (section 5.2.4); BIM AUC-ROC 0.70 (section 5.2.2); BICA AUC-ROC 0.89 (section 5.2.3); BIN precision close to 99%, estimated recall 37% on 67 known bot IDs (sections 5.1, 5.2.1); BIMAN 58 of 67 known bot IDs (Table 2). Prevalence estimate: about 1% of commit author IDs are bots (section 5.3). Note: RABBIT's related work attributes precision 0.667, recall 0.866, F1 0.754 to BIMAN; I could not find those numbers in the BIMAN paper text — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172); [RABBIT](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- Limitations stated: golden data built from BIN may miss bots that hide, no independent ground truth, ensemble trained on small data, mixed human/script accounts and shared organisational IDs, no alias merging; at 1% prevalence a 90%-accurate model yields only an 8.3% chance a flagged author is truly a bot (section 6.1) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172).

#### BotHunter (Abdellatif, Wessel, Steinmacher, Gerosa, Shihab, 2022)
- Venue: MSR 2022, pp. 6-17; DOI 10.1145/3524842.3527959 — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3524842.3527959), [author PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf).
- Task: label an account bot or human regardless of activity level — [PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf).
- Data: oracle of 5,107 accounts built from the BoDeGHa dataset and the BIMAN bots whose logins could be recovered by e-mail (300 of 461), filtering accounts with no activity since 2017; 86.7% human, 13.3% bot (Table 2, section 3.2) — [PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf).
- Features and model: 19 features in 3 dimensions (profile: login, name, bio, followers, following, account tag; activity: counts, median activities per day, median response time; text similarity of comments and commit messages, Table 1); Random Forest, best of 5 classifiers, stratified 10-fold CV, scikit-learn defaults — [PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf).
- Metrics: RF precision 95.7, recall 89.4, F1 92.4, AUC 98.3 (Table 3; abstract and conclusion state AUC 98.7, an internal inconsistency). issue_bots: F1 91.7, AUC 98.7; commit_bots: F1 89.7, AUC 95.9 (Table 4). Most important features: account name, login, followers, issue/PR comment similarity, median activity per day (section 4.2, Table 5). Against prior tools: BoDeGHa P 77.5, R 5.9, F1 11 (Table 6; text says 12.1%); BIMAN P 83.6, R 99.4, F1 90.8 on 1,938 accounts (Table 6) — [PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf).
- Limitations stated: GitHub API exposes only the top 300 activities of the last three months; 29 bots misclassified because inactive in that window; oracle inherited from earlier datasets; no separate test set; mixed accounts unresolved (sections 5, 7) — [PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf). Dataset [Zenodo 6395211](https://zenodo.org/records/6395211) (CC-BY-4.0).

#### RABBIT, first version (Chidambaram, Mens, Decan, 2024)
- Venue: MSR 2024, Data and Tool Showcase, pp. 687-691; DOI 10.1145/3643991.3644877 — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3643991.3644877), [author PDF](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- Task and data: per-account bot/human from recent public events; 1,335 labelled accounts (644 bots, 691 humans) combined from Chidambaram et al.'s activity dataset and Wyrich et al.'s list (629 accounts re-labelled by two raters, kappa 0.79, 410 bots, 189 humans, 30 discarded), after removing accounts with fewer than 5 events and ignoring GitHub Apps (section 3) — [PDF](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- Features and model: 6 activity-sequence features (distinct activity types, mean activities per type, median time between different-type activities, mean activities per repository, distinct repository owners, Gini of inter-activity time), XGBoost, best of 3,734 candidates (section 4) — [PDF](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- Metrics: test set 534 accounts: precision, recall, F1 .919, AUC .921; TP 237, FN 21, FP 21, TN 255 (Table 2); correct classification 86.3% (0,100] events, 94.2% (100,200], 98.5% above 200 (section 6.1, Table 3); BotHunter on the same set P .939, R .899, F1 .919 and 7h19m vs. RABBIT's 8 minutes (section 6.2, Table 4) — [PDF](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- Limitations stated: public events only; Events API only; cannot predict accounts not recently active; mixed accounts impossible to predict (section 6.3) — [PDF](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).

#### BIMBAS and RABBIT (Chidambaram, Decan, Mens, 2025)
- Venue: Journal of Systems and Software 221, article 112287, March 2025 (online 26 Nov 2024); DOI 10.1016/j.jss.2024.112287 — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jss.2024.112287), [preprint](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- Data: 2,150 contributors active on 3 May 2024 (1,115 humans, 1,035 bots of which 242 Apps and 793 bot accounts, sections 1 and 3; section 3 also says 791, an internal inconsistency), four sources (Table 1), kappa 0.91 (section 7); 376,638 events from the GitHub Events API, 337,246 activities, 24 activity types; test set 40% (414 bots, 446 humans) — [preprint](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- Features and model: 45 activity-sequence features (counts plus mean, median, std, IQR and Gini of temporal/count metrics, Table 5), reduced to 38 by RFE; Gradient Boosting, best of 13,021 configurations, 10-fold grid search (sections 5.2-5.4) — [preprint](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- Metrics: test bots P .883, R .911, F1 .897; humans P .915, R .888, F1 .901; weighted .899/.899/.899 (Table 7); CV weighted F1 .932, AUC .970 (Table 6). Efficiency on 860 accounts: 22 minutes, 0.112 GB, 2,426 API queries vs. BoDeGHa 7.7 h, 3.83 GB, 10,222 queries, BotHunter 20.8 h, 0.261 GB, 37,240 queries (Table 9) — [preprint](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- Limitations stated: public events only; mixed accounts cannot be excluded; ground truth biased towards bots with "bot" in the name (67.1%, 694 of 1,035), which may overestimate NBH and BotHunter; not transferable as-is to other platforms; choice of repository for BoDeGHa/BoDeGiC (section 7) — [preprint](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).

#### BDGOA (Liao, Huang, Zhang, Wu, Cheng, 2023)
- Intelligent and Converged Networks 4(3):181-197, Sept 2023; DOI 10.23919/ICN.2023.0006; 24 features in three groups (account information, account activity, text similarity) with a fine-grained classification of behavioural events and a self-similarity measure; five classifiers, Random Forest "achieves the highest F1-score of 95.83%" (abstract only; dataset size, precision, recall and AUC not stated on the page) — [SciOpen abstract page](https://www.sciopen.com/article/10.23919/ICN.2023.0006). BIMBAS authors found no replication package, dataset or tool for it — [JSS 2025, section 4.1](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).

#### BotHawk (Bi, Zhu, Wang, Xia, Khan, Pu, 2023)
- arXiv:2307.13386 (25 Jul 2023); also listed as SSRN 10.2139/ssrn.4536606 (I found no journal publication record; the preprint carries a JSS template header) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13386), [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.2139/ssrn.4536606).
- Data: 19,779 accounts with 17 features drawn from GHTorrent active repositories (Mar 2021-Mar 2022), random accounts, and BIMAN and BoDeGHa accounts, manually labelled, contributed to the OpenDigger dataset (section 3; bot/human breakdown not verified); 17 features in 5 dimensions (profile, activities, network, text similarity, time series) (section 4.3) — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.13386).
- Model and metrics: undersampling plus bagging; Bagging-RandomForest accuracy .880, precision .893, recall .871, F1 .882, AUC .947 (Table 4); abstract and section 5.3 give AUC .947 and F1 .89, and section 5.3 gives recall .806, so figures are not fully consistent across the paper; top features include tag, followers and number of issues (section 6). I found no threats-to-validity section by keyword search — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.13386).

#### BSO-GBD (Zenodo, December 2023)
- Dataset-only record (authors "Anonymous", CC-BY-4.0): 4,786 ordinary accounts, 4,325 Human and 461 Bot, each as a per-account CSV of user_name, repo_name, event_type_L1-L3, timestamp; "self-bot" App accounts are excluded from classification; no model or metrics on the page — [Zenodo 10423985](https://zenodo.org/records/10423985). #### GitHub abuse line (closest to the disappearance task)
- Du, Yang, Zhang, Duan, Wang, Hao, Li, Yang, "Understanding Promotion-as-a-Service on GitHub", ACSAC 2020, pp. 597-610, DOI 10.1145/3427228.3427258: honeypot repository with 1,023 purchased stars and forks gives 1,023 positives; 1,550 negatives from top repositories (200 high-profile, 1,350 normal); features are 14 event-type counts, star+fork count and ratio, and "average of adjacent operations pair" within 1, 2, 3 minutes and 1 hour (Table 3); SVM P 98.5%, R 99.7%, F1 0.991 under 10-fold CV (Table 4, section 3.6); applied to 23,375,824 accounts from GH Archive 2015-2019 (4.79 TB) it flags 63,872 suspected promotion accounts — [paper](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~shao/papers/du_acsac20.pdf).
- He, Yang, Burckhardt, Kapravelos, Vasilescu, Kästner, "Six Million (Suspected) Fake Stars on GitHub", ICSE 2026, DOI 10.1145/3744916.3764531: StarScout runs on GH Archive (BigQuery replica: 63.9M users, 331M repositories, 326M stars, 6.7B other events as of Jan 2025); low-activity signature (one WatchEvent plus at most one other event on the same repository and day) and lockstep signature via CopyCatch on the account-repository star bipartite graph with n=50, m=10, Δt=30 days, ρ=0.5 (sections 3.2-3.3); result 6.0M fake stars, 18,617 repositories, 301k accounts from Jul 2019-Dec 2024; limitations: evidence insufficient for disciplinary action, ad-hoc thresholds, false positives possible (section 3.5) — [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13459v2).

### Inferences
- Metrics across papers are not comparable (different labelled sets, class ratios, and whether "unknown" counts as error); only Table 7 of JSS 2025 compares on a common test set. For the SOTA table, quote that table and flag the others as "own dataset".
- Every evaluation design I read uses random or stratified splits (BoDeGHa section 5.1, RABBIT section 4, BIMBAS section 4.2, BotHunter section 3.3/RQ1, BIMAN section 5.2, Du section 3.6, BotHawk section 5.3); none groups by coordination cluster, so same-network leakage is not addressed in this literature.
- The DevBot line treats bots as benign helpers to filter out for research validity; the fraud line (Du, StarScout) treats automation as abuse. The project's disappearance target aligns more with the fraud line, whose accounts are largely non-human-developer but not "DevBots"; a 39.2% disappearance rate in the 20+ events stratum is unlikely to be explained by DevBot baselines alone.

### Gaps
- BDGOA: dataset size and precision/recall could not be verified (only the abstract page opened).
- BotHawk bot/human breakdown not verified.

## 2. What must each method observe at inference, and does any work on one hour or less or report performance versus observation length? (C3)

### Takeaway
All verified methods need long per-account histories or multiple comments/commits (5-300 events, 90-day API windows, or years of GH Archive), and none has been evaluated on a window of one hour or less. Prior work does report performance versus number of events or comments (BIMBAS Fig. 3, RABBIT Table 3, BoDeGHa Fig. 7), but I found none reporting it versus wall-clock window length, so C3 appears new as a time-based latency curve (a search-based claim, see Search log), with the event-count curves as its closest prior art.

Sources for the table: [BoDeGHa preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303) and [README](https://github.com/sgl-umons/BoDeGHa); [BIMBAS preprint, sections 2.2, 4.4-4.6 and 5.1](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf); [BIMAN preprint, section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172); [BotHunter, section 7.1](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf); [RABBIT 2024, section 5](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf); [GitHub Events docs](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/activity/events); [Du et al., Table 3](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~shao/papers/du_acsac20.pdf); [StarScout, sections 3.2-3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13459v2).

- Performance as a function of observation length (events, not time): BIMBAS misclassifies 25% of contributors on the latest 8 activities, 16% on 25 activities, and at most 10% from 107 activities onward (section 5.6, Fig. 3) — [JSS 2025](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf); RABBIT v1 correct classification 86.3% for up to 100 events, 94.2% for (100,200], 98.5% for more (section 6.1, Table 3) — [MSR 2024](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf); BoDeGHa F1 0.87 at 0-4 non-empty comments rising to 0.96-1.00 from 10 (section 5.2, Fig. 7) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- GitHub states that the Events API keeps only events from the past 30 days, up to 300 events, is "not built to serve real-time use cases", and that "event latency can be anywhere from 30s to 6h" — [GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/rest/activity/events); the BIMBAS paper reported a 90-day window as of 2024 — [JSS 2025, section 5.1](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- Local check (dump 2024-04-01-15, 64,549 non-[bot]-suffix accounts): 13.07% have at least 5 events in the hour (RABBIT's minimum); 3.94% have at least 10 events; 0.11% have at least 10 comment-type events (BoDeGHa's minimum is per repository, so this is an upper bound); 4.49% have at least 10 commits in PushEvent payloads. These are my own aggregates, not literature results.
- Adjacent, not GitHub: Panayiotou, Mannocci, Tesconi (AIDEM workshop, ECML-PKDD 2026, arXiv:2609.21959) study how window length and stride change coordinated-behaviour detection on social media, finding that "window length determines which coordination patterns are detectable" (abstract only) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21959).
- Minute-level timing features exist but over long histories: Du et al.'s adjacent-operation features at 1-3 minutes (Table 3) — [paper](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~shao/papers/du_acsac20.pdf); BIMBAS's median time between different activity types, 0.003 h for bots vs. 0.125 h for humans (Table 8) — [JSS 2025](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).

### Inferences
- C3 overlap verdict: the event-count curves (BIMBAS Fig. 3 and similar) are the closest prior art and a reviewer will likely cite them; C3's distinct contribution is the wall-clock latency framing at 60 to 5 minutes using only the public event stream. Absence of any time-window evaluation is supported by the Search log below, not proven.
- Applying BIMBAS/RABBIT to one-hour windows would leave most accounts "Unknown" (fewer than 5 events) under the default threshold, and those with few events are exactly where the published curves are weakest; the event-count evidence above suggests sharply degraded performance in the 5-25 event range, and the project's own raw-count baseline needs to be beaten there.
- Any near-real-time claim must account for GitHub's documented 30 s to 6 h event latency, which is a separate factor from the observation window.

### Gaps
- No source measures how BIMBAS-type features behave when computed only on the first 5-60 minutes of an account's activity (Search log, queries A6, O9, W1).

## 3. Can any of them run on GH Archive alone, without per-account API calls?

### Takeaway
Yes for feature computation in some cases, but none has been validated on short windows: Du et al. and StarScout detect from GH Archive only; RABBIT-ng offers an offline mode that accepts event lists (so GH Archive-derived events could be fed to the pre-trained BIMBAS model); BoDeGHa-style text features are computable from archived comments but need at least 10 comments; BIMAN's file-level features and BotHunter's profile features are not in GH Archive.

### Cited Findings
- GH Archive records "the public GitHub timeline" in hourly archives since 2011-02-12, with data from 1 January 2015 onward from the Events API — [GH Archive](https://www.gharchive.org/).
- Du et al. used four GH Archive properties (actor login, repository name, created_at, event type) for the event features, and collected registration and profile information separately via the GitHub API and web interface — [paper, section 3.1](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~shao/papers/du_acsac20.pdf).
- StarScout's detectors are SQL queries on GH Archive in BigQuery; in the validation step the GitHub API is used to check whether repositories and a 10,000-account sample (downsampled because of rate limits) were still accessible at the time of detection — [arXiv v2, sections 3.3-3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13459v2).
- RABBIT-ng's README documents "Offline usage (without API calls)" where the user passes "a list of events for each contributor" to `predict_user_type` with the pre-trained model; the normal pipeline calls the Users API for existence and type, then the Events API, and returns `Unknown` under the default 5-event threshold — [RABBIT-ng README](https://github.com/sgl-umons/RABBIT-ng). The README does not state whether the 5-event threshold is applied in offline mode.
- BotHunter needs profile information (login, name, bio, followers, following, tag), which comes from the GitHub API — [BotHunter, Table 1 and section 3.2](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf); the BIMBAS authors confirm it relies on the GitHub API — [JSS 2025, section 4.6](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- BIMAN needs commit file lists and project associations from World of Code; the World of Code dataset requires individually granted access — [BIMAN, sections 4.1-4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172); [JSS 2025, section 4.1](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- The RABBIT authors note that GH Archive is costly for per-account retrieval: hourly contributor event files range from 100 MB to 1,000 MB and finding all events for 105 days would mean processing more than 1.5 TB — [Chidambaram dissertation, May 2025](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/dissertation.pdf).
- Local check on the April 2024 dump: `actor` has only id, login, display_login, gravatar_id, url, avatar_url (no `type`, no followers/bio); IssueCommentEvent payloads carry `comment.body` (11,183 of 11,183); PushEvent payload commits carry sha, author, message, distinct, url and no file list (157,893 of 158,172 PushEvents have at least one commit). So BoDeGHa/BoDeGiC-style text features are computable from 2024 archives, while BIMAN's BICA features and BotHunter's profile features are not. These are my own aggregates.
- GitHub removed fields from Events API payloads from 7 Oct 2025 (pull request event details, push commit summaries, `author_association`), with removed data remaining available via the REST API — [GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-08-08-upcoming-changes-to-github-events-api-payloads/). This does not affect 2024 hours but affects any extension to later data.

### Inferences
- The realistic GH-Archive-only baselines for this project are: (a) raw event counts (the project's own), (b) a name-based heuristic (computable from `actor.login`), (c) RABBIT-ng offline on windowed events for the subset of accounts with at least 5 events, (d) re-implemented Du-style interval features, (e) StarScout's low-activity and lockstep signatures for WatchEvents, noting these were designed for long histories, not 5-60 minute windows.
- BIMBAS's model was trained on up to 300 events per account over up to 90 days; feeding it 5-60 minutes of events is a distribution shift that the published evaluation does not cover, so any "RABBIT-ng offline on GH Archive" baseline should be reported with that caveat.

### Gaps
- No published evaluation of RABBIT/BIMBAS, BoDeGHa or BotHunter on GH-Archive-derived event streams or sub-hour windows was found (Search log).

## 4. How is the [bot] suffix vs. undeclared automation treated, is PU learning used, and do C1/C2 overlap?

### Takeaway
The literature treats [bot]-suffixed GitHub App accounts as trivially identifiable (often removed or short-circuited by the Users API `type` field) and focuses on undeclared bot accounts on ordinary user accounts, but labels are obtained from curated lists; several papers explicitly acknowledge name-based selection bias, yet I found no work framing the problem as PU learning or correcting for a biased-label mechanism (absence is supported by the Search log). C1 partly overlaps prior work because StarScout and Du et al. checked GitHub account status retrospectively, but only for validation or descriptive analysis; neither uses numeric-ID status as a prediction target. C2 overlaps with StarScout's CopyCatch lockstep detection on the star graph, but cross-event tensors, PU modelling and cluster-grouped splits were not found.

### Cited Findings

**Treatment of Apps vs. user-account automation**
- GitHub Apps' `[bot]` suffix is "reserved" for GitHub Apps and RABBIT checks that the login ends with `[bot]` and that the Users API `type` is `Bot`; Apps were excluded from RABBIT v1's labelled dataset because they are "already marked as 'Bot' by GitHub" — [RABBIT 2024, sections 3 and 5, footnote 3](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- BIMBAS states that bot actors serving Apps "are trivial to detect through the Users API" and that GitHub does not allow distinguishing humans from bot accounts of type User, which is the motivation for bot identification; its ground truth nevertheless contains 242 Apps among 1,035 bots — [JSS 2025, sections 1 and 3](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf). I found no statement that Apps were excluded from training or test; section 5.1 computes activities for all 2,150 contributors, while the RABBIT tool short-circuits App accounts via the Users API `type` (section 6.1).
- BoDeGHa's positives were manually rated with account names hidden, but the candidate pool was enriched with 386 accounts containing strings such as "bot", "ci", "cla", "auto"; 74.7% of its bots contain "bot" in the name and 3.8% of its humans do (section 7) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303).
- BotHunter's most important features are account name and login, and 71% of bot logins contain "bot" (RQ2) — [paper](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf); the BIMBAS authors show that BotHunter without name heuristics drops from bot recall .928 to .587 (Table 7) and that 67.1% of their bots contain "bot" in the name, "biased, by construction" (section 7) — [JSS 2025](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- GitHub's own account type field has bot recall .360 and precision 1.000, and the "bot" suffix recall .520 and precision 1.000, in the BotSE 2022 study — [Table 1](https://orbi.umons.ac.be/bitstream/20.500.12907/17537/1/botse_paper.pdf).
- BDGOA specifically addresses OAuth Apps, noting existing methods rarely distinguish GitHub Apps from OAuth Apps — [SciOpen abstract](https://www.sciopen.com/article/10.23919/ICN.2023.0006). BSO-GBD excludes "self-bot" App accounts from classification — [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/10423985).
- GitHub's Terms: accounts must be created by a human, "Accounts registered by 'bots' or other automated methods are not permitted", but a machine account "is used exclusively for performing automated tasks", the owner "is ultimately responsible for the machine's actions", and a person may maintain "no more than one free machine account" in addition to a personal account — [Terms of Service, section B.3](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service). Undeclared automation on user accounts is therefore partly legitimate, which is a reason a "bot" label is not the same as "abuse".
- Mixed accounts (human and script activity on one account) are acknowledged as unresolved: excluded from BoDeGHa (78 cases, 1.5%, sections 3.3 and 7) — [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303); RABBIT calls them impossible to predict (section 6.3) — [MSR 2024](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf); BotHunter finds 14 misclassified mixed bots (section 5) — [paper](https://www.ime.usp.br/~gerosa/papers/MSR2022BotHunter.pdf); comment-level detectors struggle on them — [Cassee et al., TU/e record](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/human-bot-or-both-a-study-on-the-capabilities-of-classification-m/).
- Local check (dump 2024-04-01-15): 315 of 64,864 actor IDs (0.49%) have the `[bot]` suffix and produce 47,250 of 272,191 events (17.4%). Payload `user.type` exists for issue/PR/comment objects but describes the author of the object, not the event actor. These are my own aggregates.

**Positive-unlabeled framing**
- Du et al. use honeypot-bought accounts as positives and curated active developers as negatives — a positive/negative design, not PU — [section 3.5](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~shao/papers/du_acsac20.pdf). StarScout is unsupervised (heuristics plus CopyCatch) and explicitly calls its results "suspected" because no ground truth exists — [section 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13459v2). BIMBAS, RABBIT, BoDeGHa, BotHunter and BIMAN are fully supervised binary classifiers on labelled sets (sections cited above).
- BIMAN notes the BIN-derived golden dataset may not represent bots that hide (section 6.1) and BIMBAS discusses name bias (section 7), but neither models the labelling mechanism — [BIMAN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.03172); [JSS 2025](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf).
- No GitHub-account study framed as PU learning was found; see Search log queries A3-A5, O3, W5 (absence claim, search-based).

**C1: retrospective labels from current platform status**
- Du et al. visited 28,258 suspected promotion accounts (those from 2018, due to API limits) and found only 1,184 (4.19%) banned; they state that a banned account returns a 404 page, that a 404 cannot distinguish "does not exist" from "banned", and that they disambiguated by checking the event log (section 4.4) — [paper](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~shao/papers/du_acsac20.pdf).
- StarScout checked, via the GitHub API at detection time, whether all flagged repositories and a 10,000-account sample were still accessible: 90.42% of campaign repositories and 57.07% of campaign accounts deleted (as of January 2025), vs. baselines of 5.03% (random repositories with at least 50 stars) and 3.54% (random stargazers of those repositories), Table 1 — [arXiv v2, section 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13459v2). The study notes GitHub does not preserve deleted repositories and users, which limits analysis (section 1, section 4.3).
- BIMBAS reports that 86.1% of Wyrich et al.'s 4,654 bot accounts failed its "recently active" filter and warns that "ground truth datasets become outdated over time, including bots that may no longer exist today" (sections 2.2, 3) — [JSS 2025](https://decan.lexpage.net/files/JSS-2025.pdf); RABBIT labels non-existent accounts `Invalid` — [MSR 2024, section 5](https://natarajan-chidambaram.github.io/papers/MSR2024_RABBIT_A_tool_for_identifying_bot_accounts_based_on_their_recent_GitHub_event_history.pdf).
- A keyword search of the full text of BoDeGHa, BIMBAS, BotHunter, RABBIT 2024, Du et al., StarScout and BotHawk found no mention of numeric IDs or renames (terms: "numeric id", "user id", "account id", "node id", "renam"); their ground truths are keyed on login or name strings, for example the BoDeGHa dataset contains "the name of the repository, the name of the commenter and whether it is a bot or a human" — [BoDeGHa preprint, section 3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03303). See the Search log at the end of this file for the exact terms.

**C2: coordination structure**
- StarScout's lockstep signature applies CopyCatch to the account × repository star bipartite graph with time windows (n=50, m=10, Δt=30 days, ρ=0.5), restricted to stars — [sections 3.2-3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13459v2). Its output is a detection, not an input feature to a classifier; no PU model and no grouped splits are involved.

### Inferences
- C1 verdict: partial overlap. Retrospective status checks of GitHub accounts have been used for validation (Du 2020, StarScout 2026), but I found none that uses status at a fixed long horizon (31 months) as the prediction target, none that uses numeric IDs to avoid rename artefacts, and none that treats survivors as unlabeled. The claim should be worded narrowly (numeric-ID, prediction target, PU treatment), not as "first to use deletion status". Du's 4.19% and StarScout's 57.07% also show account-removal rates depend strongly on the population and elapsed time, supporting the project's plan to stratify by activity and to report the horizon.
- C2 verdict: StarScout overlaps on "coordination via dense bipartite blocks on GH Archive"; the cross-event-type tensor, dense blocks as PU features and cluster-grouped splits are not found in this catalogue. CopyCatch itself and general dense-subgraph/tensor methods are outside this section's scope and should be covered by the adjacent SOTA section.
- The `[bot]` suffix is a precise, low-recall label of declared automation (GitHub Apps), not a sample of "bots" in general; because the literature's bot labels are heavily enriched for names containing "bot" (67.1% in BIMBAS, 74.7% in BoDeGHa), any classifier evaluated on them inherits that selection, which is exactly the setting where PU-style reasoning would be more defensible than clean binary metrics. That reasoning is an inference, not a claim made by any cited paper.

### Gaps
- Whether GitHub's 404 on a numeric ID distinguishes deletion from suspension: not resolved by any source I read; Du et al. address it only for logins.

## 6. Is there 2024-2026 work on detecting AI coding agents or LLM-driven automation on GitHub, and is it relevant to April 2024 data?

### Takeaway
There is substantial 2025-2026 work on attributing PRs and commits to AI coding agents, using agent logins, config files, branch prefixes, co-author trailers and behavioural features, plus one dataset (CodAGE) built from GH Archive that starts in January 2024. It is largely not relevant to April 2024 labels: the agent adoption wave those works measure starts in late 2024-2025, and their signature-based labels would rarely fire on April 2024 accounts. It does show that signature-based labels undercount agents, which bears on the project's PU framing, and that signature-free detection precision is low at realistic base rates.

### Cited Findings
- AIDev (Li, Zhang, Hassan, arXiv:2507.15003, 20 Jul 2025): first large-scale dataset of agent-authored PRs, over 456,000 PRs by five agents across 61,000 repositories (abstract); PRs are found by signature queries (bot author names such as `devin-ai-integration[bot]`, branch prefixes such as `head:copilot/`, and PR-body messages such as "Co-Authored-By: Claude"); start dates are official release dates where documented, with a conservative 1 Jan 2025 for tools without one (Cursor, Copilot), and a collection cut-off of 22 Jun 2025 (section 3); Codex released 16 May 2025; dataset at [SAILResearch/AI_Teammates_in_SE3](https://github.com/SAILResearch/AI_Teammates_in_SE3) (no licence declared per GitHub API) — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15003).
- Khosravani and Mockus (arXiv:2606.24429, 23 Jun 2026): multi-method detection of agent traces across World of Code (180M+ repositories, 12 agents, snapshots Dec 2024, Oct 2025, Apr 2026) using configuration files, commit messages, author identity and bot signatures; 850,157 Claude Code commits detected in one snapshot, of which bot-account lookup recovers only 28,154 (3.3%); the December 2024 snapshot contains no hits for 2025-era agents ("The zero results reflect the December 2024 snapshot boundary"; section on temporal boundary) — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24429).
- Ghaleb (MSR 2026, pp. 822-826, DOI 10.1145/3793302.3793582; arXiv:2601.17406): classifies which agent produced a PR among five agents using 41 features from AIDev, 33,580 PRs, 97.2% F1 multi-class; explicitly motivated by undisclosed agent use under human accounts; multi-class attribution, not bot-vs-human detection — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17406).
- Horikawa et al. (arXiv:2610.36229, 26 Oct 2026): signature-free commit detection on 7,837,946 commits from 1,226 repositories; ROC AUC up to 0.859, but at the observed agent share (0.059) the best detector reaches precision only 0.332 on the full test set; transfer to held-out Claude Code gives AUC 0.524 (abstract and section 1); the study's "LLM era" ends at the earliest PR by a studied agent in AIDev, opened by Devin on 24 December 2024, and its "Agent era" runs from June 2025, after the public previews of the Copilot coding agent (19 May 2025) and Google Jules (20 May 2025), to August 2026 (section 3.1) — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36229).
- Robbes, Matricon, Degueule, Hora, Zacchiroli, "Agentic Much?", TOSEM 35(11), 2026, DOI 10.1145/3822180: "In the first half of 2025, coding agents have emerged as a category"; adoption 22.20-28.66% of projects on 21 Feb 2026 in a 128,018-project sample (abstract only) — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3822180).
- Selvanayagam and Ghaleb, "AI-to-AI Code Reviews" (ESEM 2026; arXiv:2608.21311): uses CodAGE, which is collected from GHArchive with a snapshot from 2024-01-01 to 2026-04-15, and notes "many autonomous AI coding agents emerged in early 2025" — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21311).
- CodAGE dataset card: 27,085,446 events (18.1 GB), 16 agents including Copilot, Claude Code, Devin, Cursor, Codex, CodeRabbit, Sweep, Aider, SWE-agent; attribution by "an exact bot login, a PR head-branch prefix, or a substring in any text field", explicitly "a candidate attribution, not a verified label" and "deliberately over-inclusive"; derived from GH Archive, CC BY 4.0 (dataset card, no earliest-agent date stated beyond the collection window) — [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/taher-ghaleb/CodAGE).
- BotSE 2026's accepted list contains no paper on bot or agent account detection (page summary) — [BotSE 2026](https://conf.researchr.org/home/icse-2026/botse-2026).

### Inferences
- Relevance to April 2024 is low for labelling: agent-signature methods depend on logins, branch prefixes and trailers that the cited works themselves place after December 2024 (Horikawa: earliest AIDev agent PR on 24 Dec 2024; AIDev start dates in late 2024 or 2025 with a 22 Jun 2025 cut-off; census December 2024 snapshot empty for 2025-era agents). A CodAGE-style attribution on April 2024 would at most surface tools active then (for example Sweep, Aider, SWE-agent or PR-Agent traces, all in CodAGE's agent list), not the 2025 agent wave; this could be checked directly on CodAGE's 2024 slice.
- The census's 30-fold gap between bot-account lookup and multi-method detection is direct evidence that declared bot accounts are a biased and incomplete positive set, analogous to the `[bot]`-suffix situation in question 4; it supports the project's PU framing even though it concerns a later period.
- For April 2024 accounts, LLM-driven automation is possible but cannot be labelled by these signatures; undeclared LLM automation at that date should be stated as an unmeasured confound, not as absent.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies agent or LLM-driven account activity on GitHub specifically in April 2024.
- Whether accounts flagged in 2024 CodAGE events later disappear is untested.
